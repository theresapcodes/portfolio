Dashboards in Fullstory

Fullstory’s first canvas for creating, monitoring, and sharing custom data stories.

From kickoff to launch

Customers were building dashboards by hand

Customers could search and watch user behavior in Fullstory, but they had no good way to share what they found. Interviews showed them watching upwards of 300 sessions to analyze behavior. They kept several Fullstory tabs open to compare data and pasted screenshots, charts, and text into makeshift dashboards of their own.

The pain points were consistent across interviews:

  • They couldn’t change how data cards were displayed. “If I can’t get data out, and change the way it looks, how am I supposed to use it?”
  • Comparing data was hard. “I can’t make a decision based off of just a single search.”
  • Executives had no place to check in. “We need a place where we can see the things we care about in one view.”

The business case was just as clear. As Fullstory moved upmarket, dashboards had become table stakes in head-to-head deals, and several major accounts asked for them directly. The goal was to give customers a canvas and tools to create, monitor, and share data stories without leaving Fullstory, and to close the gap with competitors in the emerging product analytics space.

What customers told us

2020

We started with two user groups. Sales Engineers demo Fullstory and show prospects what it can do. Advocates are the power users who champion Fullstory inside their organizations and teach others how to use it.

We interviewed customers who had already asked about dashboards. We asked what problems brought them to Fullstory, which teams wanted dashboards and why, what dashboard products they used today, and whether they used dashboards to triage issues.

Three findings shaped the scope:

  • Every team wanted them. “Every team has a request for it.” Product teams wanted to troubleshoot, monitor feature rollouts, and see how issues affected end users.
  • Competitors set the bar for sharing. One customer described a competitor’s dashboards arriving as emailed reports with charts and timelines, broken down by time range, location, device, and browser.
  • Triage depended on comparison. Customers used geolocation dashboards to find where an issue was happening, shared them with engineering, and wanted to see the same metric across multiple segments.

Those conversations pointed V.0 toward a high-level executive dashboard, with the ability to compare multiple lines on a trend card.

Shipping a minimum lovable product in 9 to 12 weeks

2020

We committed to getting Dashboards behind a feature flag in 9 to 12 weeks, so real customer feedback could shape everything that came after. The deadline drove every scoping decision.

Our PM, Tommy, built a MoSCoW sheet (Must, Should, Could, Won’t) that gave every idea a place to be heard and logged. It also made us weigh priority against feasibility, and showed which features made sense to release together.

Our goal for V.0 was a minimum lovable product with one primary use case, a high-level executive dashboard. Customers should be able to complete workflows more easily in Fullstory, know where to begin, customize dashboards to their business, and compare data to make decisions.

In V.0

  • Dashboards you can create, rename, duplicate, delete, and edit
  • Four card types with the same controls: funnel, trend, single metric, and text
  • Comparison of two or more lines on a trend card
  • Titles and descriptions on cards, so owners can add an observation
  • Reordering cards within a dashboard
  • Designed states for first-time users, returning users, empty dashboards, and populated ones

Left for later

  • Drag and drop, inline editing, and other nice-to-haves that needed more technical lift than the timeline allowed

The point of V.0 was to get it out and iterate on customer feedback.

Keeping other teams in the loop

2020

Dashboards touched many teams, so I wrote a documentation deck that kept everyone working from the same plan.

Session replay was what Fullstory was known for. Dashboards and Metrics showed where the product was heading, which meant planning drew interest and input from across the company. With an aggressive timeline, there was little room for surprises. I worked in the open and circulated progress often.

The deck did several jobs over the course of design, development, and incremental release. It kept the core team aligned. It gave designers, engineers, and PMs on other teams a place to raise questions where their work overlapped with ours. It also let everyone else in the company follow the latest developments and plan around upcoming releases.

From Dashboards to custom metrics

2020

V.0 gave users the basics of creating and managing a dashboard. V.1 focused on customization, starting with tools for configuring their own ad hoc custom metrics.

Metrics did not exist as their own entity in Fullstory yet, so custom metrics were only available inside Dashboards at first. We launched Metrics as a standalone feature the following quarter, and kept collecting feedback and iterating on Dashboards alongside it.

The V.1 and V.2 scope covered:

  • Metric configuration
  • Conversion rate workflows
  • User trend workflows
  • Dimensionality workflows
  • Continued iteration on features already launched

Outcomes

Dashboards launched to all Fullstory customers in July 2020, just under six months after the work began. Customers now had a canvas and tools to create, monitor, and share metrics with their teams, and it moved Fullstory toward product analytics. Metrics followed as a standalone feature the next quarter.

Reflection

The 9 to 12 week limit forced us to decide what mattered. We couldn’t build everything, so every request had to earn its place. The MoSCoW sheet made those tradeoffs a shared conversation, and leaving out drag and drop and inline editing in V.0 let us ship sooner and learn from real use.

Working in the open mattered as much. The documentation deck meant teams across the company knew what was coming and how it affected them, which kept a fast timeline from turning into surprises.
